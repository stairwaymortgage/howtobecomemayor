What Does a Mayor Do

Jim BlackburnMarch 20268 min read

What does a mayor do? The honest answer is that it depends significantly on which form of government your city uses — and understanding the difference is one of the most important things a future mayor can learn before they run.

Most people who want to become mayor have a general sense of what the job involves — cutting ribbons, presiding over city meetings, representing the community on the news. The reality is more complex, more demanding, and more consequential than any of those images suggest. Understanding what a mayor does on a daily basis is essential preparation for anyone who wants to do the job well.

According to U.S. Census Bureau data, there are 19,495 incorporated municipalities in America — each with elected leadership whose powers vary by city charter. According to the National League of Cities, the specific powers and duties of a mayor vary significantly depending on the form of government the city uses. In a strong-mayor city, the mayor functions as the chief executive with direct administrative authority over city departments. In a council-manager city — which accounts for over 60 percent of American municipalities — the mayor is primarily a legislative leader who sets vision and builds consensus rather than managing day-to-day operations.

Understanding what a mayor does in your specific city requires knowing which system your city uses. That distinction shapes everything about the job — from how much power the mayor has to how they should campaign for it.

The Strong Mayor vs. Council-Manager Distinction

In a strong mayor city, the mayor is the chief executive. They appoint and can fire department heads. They prepare and submit the city budget. They have veto power over ordinances passed by the city council. They direct city staff and set administrative priorities. Cities like New York, Chicago, Los Angeles, and Houston use variations of this model. The mayor in a strong-mayor city is functionally similar to a CEO — with the accountability that comes from being elected rather than hired.

In a council-manager city, the mayor is one vote among five or seven on the city commission. A professional city manager — hired by and accountable to the commission — runs the day-to-day operations of city government. The mayor chairs commission meetings, represents the city publicly, and leads the legislative agenda through coalition-building rather than executive authority. Fort Lauderdale, like most mid-size American cities, uses this model.

What a mayor does in a council-manager city is fundamentally about relationships and persuasion. You cannot order a department to do something. You have to convince four colleagues to vote with you. That reality makes the trust-building work of the 14-year arc to becoming mayor not just strategically smart — it is directly relevant to how the job actually functions once you have it.

What a Mayor Does on a Daily and Weekly Basis

The daily reality of a mayor's life in a mid-size American city is a combination of ceremonial obligations, legislative work, community engagement, and strategic leadership. Here is a realistic picture of what that looks like across a typical week.

Commission meetings and legislative work. Most city commissions meet two to four times per month. Each meeting requires preparation — reviewing staff reports, understanding the policy implications of agenda items, consulting with city staff and community stakeholders, and coordinating with fellow commissioners on positions. For a conscientious mayor this preparation takes significantly more time than the meeting itself.

Community engagement. A mayor who is doing the job well spends significant time in the community — attending neighborhood events, meeting with business leaders, visiting city facilities, listening to residents at town halls and informal gatherings. This is not ceremonial. It is intelligence-gathering that makes better governance possible. The mayor who knows which neighborhoods are struggling with flooding, which businesses are struggling with permitting, and which communities feel underserved makes better decisions than the mayor who only hears from organized lobbyists.

Media and public communication. What a mayor does includes a significant amount of public communication — press releases, interviews, social media, public statements. The mayor is the public face of city government. How they communicate shapes how residents understand and trust city leadership. Personal branding for civic leaders is not vanity — it is a core competency of effective mayoral leadership.

"The mayor who understands their city's power structure, builds genuine relationships across all community groups, and communicates clearly and consistently is the mayor who gets things done — regardless of which form of government their city uses."

The Most Important Decision a Mayor Makes

Ask experienced mayors what decision matters most and the answer is almost always the same: who they appoint. In a strong-mayor city, the mayor directly appoints department heads — the police chief, the fire chief, the planning director, the public works director. In a council-manager city, the mayor influences the selection of the city manager, who in turn leads the professional staff.

These appointments have consequences that outlast any single term. A police chief serves for years. A city manager shapes organizational culture across the entire city workforce. A planning director's decisions affect the built environment for decades. The mayor who appoints excellent professional leaders and trusts them to do their jobs creates a legacy that continues long after the mayor leaves office.

According to the International City/County Management Association, the relationship between an elected commission and a professional city manager is the single most important factor in the effectiveness of council-manager city governance. A mayor who understands that relationship and manages it constructively produces significantly better outcomes than one who tries to micromanage professional staff.

What a Mayor Cannot Do — And Why It Matters

As important as understanding what a mayor does is understanding what a mayor cannot do — particularly in a council-manager city. A mayor cannot unilaterally spend city money, hire or fire city staff below the manager level, direct departments to take specific actions, or make binding commitments on behalf of the city without commission approval.

These limitations are not weaknesses. They are the design of a system intended to prevent the concentration of power in a single elected official. The mayor who works effectively within these constraints — building consensus, persuading colleagues, developing genuine community trust — accomplishes far more than a mayor who tries to act unilaterally and loses the confidence of the commission and professional staff.

This is why building a political coalition is not just a campaign strategy. It is the fundamental operational skill of effective mayoral leadership. The future mayor who spends a decade learning how to build genuine trust and consensus in their community is not just preparing to win an election. People Also Ask
What is the difference between a strong mayor and a weak mayor system?
In a strong-mayor system the mayor is the chief executive with direct administrative authority — appointing department heads, preparing budgets, and directing city operations. In a weak-mayor or council-manager system the mayor is one vote on the commission and a professional city manager handles administration. The difference between a mayor and a city manager explains this in detail.
How much does a mayor get paid in a mid-size American city?
Mayoral salaries in mid-size American cities of 50,000 to 500,000 people typically range from $50,000 to $150,000 annually. Fort Lauderdale's mayor earns approximately $100,000 per year. Larger cities pay significantly more — New York City's mayor earns $258,000. Many small-city mayors serve part-time and earn $20,000 to $40,000 annually.
Does a mayor have veto power?
In strong-mayor cities, yes — the mayor typically has veto power over ordinances passed by the city council, subject to override by a supermajority of the council. In council-manager cities, the mayor generally does not have a separate veto power — they vote as one member of the commission on all legislation.
What is the most important decision a mayor makes?
Appointments. The people a mayor places in key leadership positions — city manager, department heads, advisory board members — shape city governance for years beyond any single mayoral term. A great appointment creates lasting positive impact. A poor appointment can undermine an entire administration.
How does a mayor work with a city manager?
In a council-manager city the mayor and commission set policy direction while the city manager implements it through professional staff. The relationship works best when the mayor provides clear strategic direction and trusts the manager to handle operational execution. Understanding the real power structure in a city includes this relationship at its center.
How many hours a week does a mayor work?
Full-time mayors of mid-size cities typically work 50 to 70 hours per week — commission meetings, community events, media obligations, staff consultations, and constituent services. Part-time mayors of smaller cities may work 20 to 30 hours per week while maintaining other professional commitments.
What happens when a mayor and city commission disagree?
In a council-manager city the commission governs by majority vote — if the mayor cannot persuade a majority of commissioners to support their position, the majority position prevails. This is why coalition-building is the central skill of effective mayoral leadership. Building a political coalition starts years before election day.
How does a mayor influence city budget decisions?
In strong-mayor cities the mayor typically prepares and submits the budget proposal. In council-manager cities the city manager prepares the budget with commission input and the commission approves it by majority vote. A mayor with strong relationships on the commission and deep knowledge of community priorities has significant informal influence over budget priorities regardless of which system is used. The issues that matter most in mayoral elections are usually the ones that show up most prominently in city budgets.
