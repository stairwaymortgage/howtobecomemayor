The First Step to Becoming Mayor

Jim BlackburnMarch 20268 min read

The first step to becoming mayor is simpler than almost every future candidate expects — and more powerful than almost any of them realize. It does not require money, connections, or political experience. It requires showing up to your city commission meeting and paying attention.

The first step to becoming mayor is not filing paperwork, not hiring a consultant, and not deciding which issues you want to champion. The first step to becoming mayor is attending your city commission meeting this week and sitting in the audience. That is it. That is the beginning of everything.

According to the International City/County Management Association, over 60 percent of American cities operate under a council-manager form of government, where the mayor leads through relationships and coalition-building rather than direct executive authority. That structural reality makes showing up early and consistently the single most powerful strategic investment a future mayor can make.

Most people who want to become mayor spend months preparing to get involved before they actually get involved. They read about local government. They follow the news. They tell themselves they will start attending meetings once they feel ready. That waiting is a mistake. The first step to becoming mayor is not preparation for action. It is action. Specifically, it is the act of showing up in public before you have any title, any campaign, or anything to gain from being there.

Why Attendance Is the First Step and Not a Preliminary to It

Local politics runs entirely on recognition. The neighborhood association president who has seen your face at six consecutive commission meetings treats you completely differently than someone she has never seen before. The city commissioner who watches you ask a thoughtful question at a public hearing remembers your name the next time it comes up. The civic leader who spots you at every community forum starts to think of you as someone serious. None of that recognition happens from a distance. It requires physical presence over time.

The first step to becoming mayor is also the hardest psychologically because it requires you to show up without credentials, without a platform, and without any obvious return on the investment. You are the person in the room who is clearly there to learn. That humility — showing up as a student in public before you are ready to lead — is not weakness. It is the foundation of the kind of trust that eventually wins elections. Becoming mayor with no experience starts exactly here.

The 90-Day Starter Plan — Your First Three Months

The first step to becoming mayor is simple. What comes next requires a structured plan. Here is a realistic 90-day starter plan for someone beginning from zero.

Weeks one and two. Find your city commission meeting schedule on the city website. Attend your first meeting. Bring a notebook. Observe who speaks, what issues dominate, how commissioners interact with each other and with residents. Do not speak yet. Just watch. Read your city's most recent strategic plan, which is almost always available on the city website under the city manager's office.

Weeks three and four. Find your neighborhood association and attend their next meeting. Introduce yourself simply — your name, your neighborhood, and that you are trying to learn how your city works. Ask one question. Listen more than you talk. According to the National League of Cities, neighborhood associations are among the most direct connections between residents and city decision-making — and the future mayor who earns their trust early builds a political foundation that money cannot replicate.

Weeks five and six. Attend a second commission meeting. This time, sign up for public comment. Speak for two to three minutes on a topic related to your city's most pressing issue. Be constructive, be brief, be specific. The goal is not to impress anyone. The goal is to have your name associated with engagement on a real issue, on the record, in a public meeting.

Weeks seven through twelve. Research which city advisory boards align with your signature issue. Complete and submit an application. Attend at least one public meeting of that board as an observer before your application is reviewed. Begin publishing one piece of content per week about what you are learning — a social media post, a short video, a newsletter entry. Start small. Start now. Getting involved in local government at the advisory board level is the bridge between citizen and civic leader.

What You Are Actually Building in the First Step

When you attend your city commission meeting for the first time, you are not building a campaign. You are building something more valuable — a habit of presence. The habit of showing up whether or not it is convenient. Whether or not there is a camera. Whether or not anyone important is watching.

That habit, sustained over years, becomes the most powerful political asset you will ever have. By the time you run for office, you will not need to introduce yourself to civic leaders — they will already know you. You will not need to prove you care about the city — you will have years of public record demonstrating it. You will not need to manufacture credibility — you will have earned it slowly, consistently, and visibly.

Research from the Pew Research Center consistently shows that public trust in government institutions is at historic lows — which means the civic leader who earns genuine community trust through consistent presence has a rare and powerful advantage over career politicians who have never had to build trust from scratch.

The first step to becoming mayor is simple enough to take today. Go to your city website. Find the commission meeting schedule. Put the next meeting on your calendar. Show up. That is how it starts. That is how it has always started for the people who eventually win. Should You Tell Anyone You Want to Become Mayor

Not yet. In the first step and the months that follow, your goal is to show up and learn — not to position yourself as a candidate. The moment you tell people you want to become mayor before you have built any credibility in the community, you are asking them to evaluate a claim that has no evidence behind it. That is a weak position.

The right time to tell people you are considering running is after you have already been visibly engaged in civic life for at least two to three years. At that point the announcement is not a claim — it is a confirmation of something people have already been observing. The future mayor who has been showing up for three years and then announces their intention is met with recognition. The future mayor who announces on day one is met with skepticism. Building credibility to run for office always comes before the announcement — and it always starts with the first step of showing up.

People Also Ask
What should I do in my first month of pursuing a mayoral run?
Attend your city commission meeting, find your neighborhood association, and begin reading your city's strategic plan. Do not announce anything. Do not tell people you want to run. Just show up and learn. The first month is about building the habit of presence — everything else grows from there. Here is how to get involved in local government as a complete beginner.
Do I need to announce my intentions before I start preparing?
No — and announcing too early is one of the most common mistakes future mayors make. Show up for at least two years before you tell anyone you are considering running. By then the announcement is a confirmation of what people have already observed, not a claim that has no evidence behind it.
How do I find out when my city's next mayoral election is?
Check your city clerk's website or your state's division of elections website. Every incorporated city publishes its election calendar. The requirements to run for mayor including election dates vary by city and state but are always publicly available.
What is a city commission meeting and why should I attend one?
A city commission meeting is the regular public gathering where elected city commissioners vote on legislation, budgets, zoning decisions, and policy. It is the most important room in local government — and attending as an observer is free, open to the public, and the single best education in how your city actually works.
How do I introduce myself to civic leaders without seeming political?
Simply. Your name, your neighborhood, and that you are trying to understand how your city works. Do not mention running for anything. Civic leaders are far more receptive to a curious resident than to someone who immediately signals political ambition. Curiosity is credible. Ambition without a track record is not.
Should I tell people I want to become mayor before I am ready to run?
No. Wait until you have at least two to three years of visible civic engagement behind you. The announcement lands completely differently when people have already been watching you show up. Becoming known in your community always comes before telling them you want to lead it.
How do I start learning about my city's most important issues?
Start with three sources: your city's strategic plan, your city's annual budget, and your local newspaper. The strategic plan tells you what the city says it is working on. The budget tells you what it is actually spending money on. The newspaper tells you what residents are angry about. Identifying the most important issues in your city is the foundation of your entire civic platform.
What is the 90-day starter plan for future mayors?
Attend your first commission meeting in weeks one and two. Join your neighborhood association in weeks three and four. Speak at a commission meeting for the first time in weeks five and six.
