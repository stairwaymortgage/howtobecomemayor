How to Get Your Name on the Ballot

Jim BlackburnMarch 20267 min read

Getting your name on the ballot is the first official legal step in any mayoral campaign — and the process is far more accessible than most first-time candidates expect once they understand exactly what is required.

Getting your name on the ballot is the first official legal step in any mayoral campaign — and it is more accessible than most residents expect. The process varies by city and state but follows a consistent pattern across most American municipalities: meet the eligibility requirements, file the required paperwork with the city clerk, and either pay a filing fee or submit a petition with the required number of valid voter signatures. That is the entire legal framework for getting your name on the mayoral ballot.

What makes getting your name on the ballot feel complicated to first-time candidates is not the process itself but the surrounding requirements — campaign finance reporting that must begin before you file, residency and registration requirements that must be met in advance, and petition signature gathering that requires organized effort if you choose that route. Understanding each of these components clearly eliminates the mystery and reveals a straightforward bureaucratic process that any organized candidate can navigate.

According to the U.S. Election Assistance Commission, candidacy requirements for local office vary significantly by state and municipality — which means the specific steps to get your name on the mayoral ballot in your city require research into your city clerk's specific procedures rather than reliance on general guidance alone.

The Filing Process — Step by Step

Step one: Confirm your eligibility. Before anything else, verify that you meet the basic candidacy requirements for your specific city — residency duration, voter registration status, and any age requirements. Your city clerk's website is the authoritative source. The complete requirements to run for mayor covers the standard eligibility criteria in detail.

Step two: Establish your campaign committee. In most states, you must designate a campaign treasurer and establish a campaign committee before accepting any contributions or making any expenditures — including before paying a filing fee with campaign funds. This step must come first. Contact your city clerk or a local election attorney to understand the specific committee formation requirements in your jurisdiction.

Step three: Open a campaign bank account. All campaign funds must flow through a dedicated campaign bank account that is separate from any personal or business accounts. Commingling campaign funds with personal funds is a campaign finance violation in virtually every jurisdiction. Open the account in the name of your campaign committee as soon as your committee is officially formed.

Step four: Obtain the candidate filing packet. Go to your city clerk's office or their website and request the candidate filing packet for the mayor's race. This packet contains the official forms required to place your name on the ballot — typically including a declaration of candidacy, a financial disclosure form, and instructions for the filing fee or petition signature process.

Filing Fee vs. Petition Signatures — Which to Choose

Most cities offer candidates a choice between paying a filing fee or collecting a required number of valid petition signatures from registered voters in the city. Understanding the tradeoffs helps you choose the right approach for your specific campaign situation.

Filing fees are straightforward — a payment made to the city clerk that places your name on the ballot. For mayoral races in mid-size American cities, filing fees typically range from $500 to $5,000 depending on the city. Paying a filing fee is administratively simple and eliminates the risk of petition signature rejection. The downside is the cost — for a candidate with a lean campaign budget, a filing fee represents a significant early expenditure.

Petition signatures require gathering a specified number of valid signatures from registered voters in your city — typically between 500 and 2,000 signatures for a mayoral race in a mid-size city. Petition gathering is free in terms of financial cost but requires significant organized effort — typically two to four weeks of canvassing and volunteer coordination to gather enough valid signatures to comfortably exceed the requirement.

The petition route has a hidden strategic advantage: the process of gathering signatures is also a voter contact operation. Every signature conversation is an opportunity to introduce yourself as a candidate and make a positive first impression on a registered voter. A campaign that gathers 1,500 signatures in two weeks has contacted 1,500 voters directly — a meaningful head start on the campaign's voter contact goals. Winning a local election with no name recognition can begin with the petition process itself.

"Petition gathering is the only campaign activity that is legally required to be completed before your campaign officially begins — and the only one that produces both ballot access and direct voter contact simultaneously."

Filing Deadlines — The Most Important Date in Your Campaign Calendar

The filing deadline — the date by which all candidacy paperwork must be submitted to the city clerk — is the most important date in any campaign calendar. Miss it and you cannot run. There are no extensions, no exceptions, and no workarounds. The filing deadline is absolute.

Filing deadlines for mayoral races typically fall 60 to 90 days before the election — giving candidates a defined window after which no new candidates can enter the race. Some cities have qualifying periods rather than single deadlines — a window of two to four weeks during which candidates must file. Others have a single day by which all paperwork must be received.

Find your city's filing deadline early — ideally six to twelve months before the election — and build your entire campaign timeline backward from it. The filing deadline determines when you need to have your campaign committee established, your campaign account opened, and either your filing fee ready or your petition signature gathering completed. According to USA.gov's directory of local government resources, most state and local election authorities publish candidate filing information well in advance of the qualifying period.

What Happens After You File

After successfully filing your candidacy paperwork and having your submission accepted by the city clerk, several things happen that you need to be prepared for. First, your name and basic campaign information become public record — accessible to journalists, opposition researchers, and any interested voter. Your filing is typically announced on the city clerk's website and often covered by local media. Your campaign is now officially public.

Second, your campaign finance reporting obligations begin in full force. Periodic financial disclosure reports — showing all contributions received and all expenditures made — must be filed on the schedule required by your jurisdiction. Missing campaign finance filing deadlines carries financial penalties and can damage your credibility with voters and journalists who cover local elections.

Third, you become eligible for ballot access confirmation — a formal notice from the city clerk that your candidacy has been accepted and your name will appear on the ballot. Keep this documentation. It is the official confirmation that the administrative process is complete.

People Also Ask
What is the difference between a petition and a filing fee to get on the ballot?
A filing fee is a payment made to the city clerk to place your name on the ballot — straightforward but costs money. A petition requires gathering a specified number of valid voter signatures — free financially but requires organized effort. The petition route has a strategic advantage: the signature-gathering process is also a voter contact operation. The full requirements to run for mayor covers both options in detail.
How many signatures do I need to get on the mayoral ballot?
Signature requirements for mayoral races in mid-size American cities typically range from 500 to 2,000 valid signatures from registered voters in your city. The specific requirement for your city is found in your city charter or on the city clerk's website. Always gather 20 to 30 percent more signatures than required to account for invalid signatures that may be rejected during verification.
What happens if I miss the filing deadline?
You cannot run. Filing deadlines for ballot access are absolute — there are no extensions, no exceptions, and no workarounds. The only option is to wait for the next election cycle. This is why finding your city's filing deadline 6 to 12 months in advance and building your entire campaign timeline backward from it is essential planning discipline.
Who do I file my candidacy paperwork with?
Your city clerk is the primary filing authority for local races in most American cities. Some states route local candidacy filings through the county supervisor of elections or the state division of elections. Your city's official website and the city clerk's office can direct you to the correct filing authority. How local elections work covers the administrative structure of local election management.
Can I be removed from the ballot after I file?
Yes — in limited circumstances. Common reasons for ballot removal include failure to meet eligibility requirements (residency, voter registration, age), fraudulent petition signatures, campaign finance violations, or voluntary withdrawal. Valid filings from eligible candidates are rarely challenged successfully. Meeting all requirements before filing is the most reliable protection against ballot challenges.
What is a candidate filing fee and how much is it typically?
A filing fee is a payment made to the city clerk that places your name on the official election ballot. For mayoral races in mid-size American cities, filing fees typically range from $500 to $5,000. Some cities scale fees to city population size. Others have flat fees regardless of office level. Your city clerk's candidate information page will specify the exact amount. The total cost to run for mayor is significantly higher than the filing fee alone.
How early before the election do I need to file?
Most city filing deadlines fall 60 to 90 days before the election — though some cities have qualifying windows of two to four weeks that open and close earlier. Find your city's specific deadline from the city clerk's website and plan to file at least two weeks before the deadline to allow time to address any administrative issues with your submission.
What is a campaign treasurer and do I need one before I file?
A campaign treasurer is the individual responsible for managing and reporting campaign finances — tracking all contributions received and all expenditures made, and filing the required campaign finance disclosure reports. In most states, designating a campaign treasurer and establishing a formal campaign committee must happen before you accept any contributions or make any expenditures, which means before you pay a filing fee with campaign funds. The requirements to run for mayor include campaign finance compliance from day one.
