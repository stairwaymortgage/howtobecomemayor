Most Important People to Know in Local Politics

Jim BlackburnMarch 20268 min read

The most important people to know in local politics are not primarily the major donors or well-connected party officials — they are the civic connectors whose deep community trust is directly translatable into the electoral support that wins mayoral elections.

Local political influence is not primarily financial or institutional. It is relational. The person most influential in a neighborhood is the one whose judgment the most people trust when making decisions about local affairs. That person is often not the wealthiest or most prominent. They are the most genuinely trusted. According to Pew Research Center research on civic trust, Americans trust local civic leaders significantly more than elected politicians — which means the civic leaders who exist outside the formal political structure are often more influential with voters than the politicians themselves.

The candidate who knows the right people — and is genuinely known and trusted by them — arrives at their campaign with a head start that no advertising budget can replicate. Building the right relationships before the campaign begins is the most reliable electoral investment a future mayor can make.

Most candidates invest their relationship energy in the wrong direction — seeking out the most prominent or most powerful people first, rather than the most trusted. This is the mistake that produces shallow political networks that look impressive but deliver little on election day.

The Five Most Important Relationships for a Future Mayor

Neighborhood association presidents. The presidents of your city's most active neighborhood associations collectively represent the most organized, most engaged, and most politically active segment of local voters. Building genuine relationships with these leaders — the kind that comes from years of working alongside them on community issues — is the single most valuable relationship investment available to a future mayor. In a city with 15 neighborhood associations, the candidate who has genuine relationships with 10 of their presidents has a structural electoral advantage that is very difficult to overcome. How neighborhood associations influence elections explains why these relationships are so valuable.

City staff at the director level. Department directors — planning director, public works director, parks director, police chief, fire chief — are career professionals with deep institutional knowledge of how the city actually works. They serve multiple mayors and administrations and maintain relationships across political transitions. A future mayor who has built genuine professional relationships with key department directors through advisory board service and commission work arrives in office with institutional knowledge that makes governance immediately more effective.

The city manager. In council-manager cities — the majority of American municipalities — the city manager is the most powerful unelected official in the city. A future mayor who has developed a respectful, productive working relationship with the city manager before taking office has a governance advantage that most first-term mayors never achieve. The difference between a mayor and city manager explains why this relationship is so consequential.

Local journalists and editors. The reporters and editors who cover local government have significant influence over how candidates are perceived by the broader voting public. A candidate who has been a reliable, knowledgeable, and honest source for local journalists covering city issues for years before their campaign has earned credibility that benefits them enormously during the race. Local journalists value sources who call proactively with genuinely newsworthy information and who are honest about what they know and do not know.

The business community connector. In every city there are one or two people who serve as central nodes of the business community network — typically the chamber of commerce president, a prominent developer, or a business association leader whose relationships span the full range of local commercial activity. A genuine relationship with the central business community connector opens doors to that entire network's fundraising capacity and civic influence.

City Staff — The People Most Candidates Overlook

City staff are among the most important and most overlooked relationships in local politics. Most candidates focus exclusively on elected officials and civic leaders — the visible faces of local political power. They underinvest in relationships with the professional staff who actually implement policy, prepare the information commissioners rely on, and maintain institutional memory across multiple elected administrations.

The planning analyst who has been working on housing policy for eight years knows more about practical constraints and opportunities than any elected official. The stormwater engineer managing flood infrastructure for a decade understands the capital investment backlog in ways no amount of meeting attendance can replace. These staff professionals are accessible to any resident who engages with them respectfully through public meetings and advisory board service.

According to the National League of Cities, the most effective first-term mayors are consistently those who arrive with established professional relationships with key city staff — because those relationships provide the institutional knowledge and operational trust that makes governance effective from day one. What a mayor does in a council-manager city is substantially about managing this relationship with professional staff.

How to Get Introduced to Key Civic Leaders

Getting introduced to key civic leaders is both simpler and slower than most people expect. Simpler because most are accessible through public civic channels — commission meetings, neighborhood association events, chamber gatherings, and nonprofit board meetings where any resident can participate. Slower because the relationships that matter are built over years, not engineered in a single introduction.

Being introduced by a mutual trusted contact is significantly more effective than cold introductions. When someone both you and a key civic leader already trust makes a specific introduction — you need to meet this person, they have been doing serious work on the flooding issue — the quality of the new relationship is better from the first moment than a cold encounter at a public meeting.

According to ICMA research on civic networks, the most effective civic leaders are those who have invested in relationships across multiple community constituencies rather than concentrating on the most visible or most prominent figures. Becoming a community leader naturally generates mutual-contact introduction opportunities as your own network expands over time.

Who Actually Controls the Most Votes

No single person controls votes directly. What civic leaders control is trust — the depth of relationship with their communities that makes their endorsement, communication, and mobilization efforts influential with specific voter constituencies. In most mid-size American cities that means: the presidents of the three or four most active neighborhood associations, faith community leaders with the largest and most civically engaged congregations, nonprofit directors whose organizations serve the most residents, and labor union leaders in cities with significant union membership.

Candidates who build genuine relationships with this distributed network of trusted civic leaders — rather than concentrating on a small number of major donors or political power brokers — consistently outperform candidates who follow the traditional political relationship model. People Also Ask
Who are the five most important people to know before running for mayor?
Neighborhood association presidents of the most active associations. The city manager. Key city department directors in your signature issue areas. The central business community connector. And local journalists who cover city government. Becoming known in your community naturally builds these relationships over time through consistent civic engagement.
How do I get introduced to key civic leaders in my city?
Show up at the places where they already gather — commission meetings, neighborhood association events, chamber gatherings. Contribute genuinely. Be introduced by mutual trusted contacts when possible. Civic organizations for political credibility are the primary venues where these introductions happen naturally over years.
What is the role of a city manager in local political relationships?
The city manager is the most powerful unelected official in a council-manager city — running day-to-day operations, supervising all departments, preparing the budget recommendation, and implementing all commission-approved policy. A future mayor with a productive relationship with the city manager before taking office governs more effectively from day one. The difference between a mayor and city manager explains why.
How important are local journalists to a mayoral campaign?
Very. Journalists who cover city government have significant influence over how candidates are perceived. A candidate who has been a reliable, honest, knowledgeable source for local journalists for years before their campaign has earned credibility that advertising cannot buy. Cold media relationships during campaign season are far less effective than professional familiarity built through years of substantive engagement on city issues.
What is a political kingmaker and does my city have one?
A political kingmaker is a person whose endorsement is widely understood to be decisive in local elections. Most mid-size American cities today do not have a single kingmaker in the traditional sense. Power is distributed among a network of trusted civic leaders whose collective endorsement is more influential than any single figure's support — which is why coalition-building across multiple constituencies matters more than any single high-profile endorsement.
How do business community relationships affect mayoral races?
Business community relationships provide fundraising access, professional network credibility, and signals of economic competence that matter to voters who prioritize business-friendly governance. Who controls the most votes in a typical American city?
No single person controls votes directly. The people who influence the most votes are those most trusted by the most engaged voters — typically the presidents of the most active neighborhood associations, the most influential faith community leaders, nonprofit directors serving the most residents, and labor leaders in union-dense cities. Building a political coalition means building genuine relationships with all of these constituencies.
How do I build relationships with people who have more power than me?
By contributing genuine value to their work before you need anything from them. Start from their needs rather than yours. The neighborhood association president who needs someone to chair a difficult committee benefits from your skills. The city department director who needs a community voice on a policy question values your informed perspective. Becoming a community leader positions you as someone whose relationships are built on genuine contribution.
