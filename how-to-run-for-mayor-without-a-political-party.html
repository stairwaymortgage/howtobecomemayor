How to Run for Mayor Without a Political Party

Jim BlackburnMarch 20268 min read

Running for mayor without a political party is not the exception in American local elections — it is the rule. Approximately 75 percent of American city elections are nonpartisan, meaning no party label appears on the ballot and no party nomination is required. Understanding how nonpartisan local elections work, and how to build a winning campaign without party infrastructure, is essential preparation for any future mayor.

The assumption that you need a political party behind you to run for mayor is one of the most common misconceptions among first-time candidates — and one of the most limiting. In the majority of American cities, local elections are specifically designed to be nonpartisan precisely because city governance is fundamentally about service delivery and community quality of life, not about the ideological battles that define state and national politics.

According to the National League of Cities, approximately 75 percent of American city elections are nonpartisan — meaning candidates run without party labels, no party primary is required, and voters choose based on candidate qualities rather than party signals. In these cities, running for mayor without a political party is not a disadvantage. It is simply how the system works.

Even in partisan cities — where party affiliation does appear on the ballot and party primaries determine general election candidates — it is possible to run for mayor without deep party establishment support, particularly as an outsider candidate who has built genuine community credibility through years of civic engagement. The party infrastructure that helps in a partisan city can be partially replicated through the civic organization network that the 14-year arc builds.

How Nonpartisan Local Elections Actually Work

In a nonpartisan local election, the ballot lists all candidates by name without party labels. Voters choose between candidates based on their knowledge of the candidates from direct experience, community networks, candidate forums, local media coverage, and whatever campaign outreach has reached them. There is no party voting cue — no shortcut that allows a voter to simply choose the candidate from their preferred party without knowing anything about that specific individual.

This absence of party voting cues is structurally advantageous for well-prepared outsider candidates. In a partisan election, even a weak incumbent can benefit from voters who support their party regardless of the candidate's specific record. In a nonpartisan election, every vote must be earned individually — through name recognition, trust, endorsements, or direct voter contact. The candidate who has built the most genuine community trust has the most direct path to those individual votes.

Nonpartisan elections also typically produce lower overall turnout than partisan elections — because the party mobilization infrastructure that drives partisan voter turnout does not exist. This lower turnout environment further advantages the candidate with the deepest community relationships, because the voters who show up in low-turnout local elections are disproportionately the most civically engaged — exactly the voters that years of neighborhood association participation, advisory board service, and community leadership most directly reach. Winning Without Party Infrastructure

Party infrastructure in a partisan election provides three things that a campaign otherwise has to build from scratch: a voter mobilization operation, a donor network, and an endorsement credibility signal. Running for mayor without a political party means building functional alternatives to each of these.

Voter mobilization without party infrastructure is replaced by civic organization mobilization — the neighborhood association networks, the faith community networks, the nonprofit organization networks, and the professional association networks that a candidate with years of genuine civic engagement has built. These networks are often more effective than party voter mobilization because they are personally motivated rather than institutionally obligated. The neighborhood association president who mobilizes her 400 members for a candidate she genuinely respects is more effective per member reached than a party precinct captain running a routine get-out-the-vote operation.

A donor network without party access is replaced by the personal and civic relationship network that years of authentic community engagement produces. Fundraising for a local political campaign without party support starts with the same first step it starts with in any campaign: the 20 people who know you best and believe in you most.

Party endorsement credibility without party affiliation is replaced by civic organization endorsements — neighborhood association endorsements, chamber of commerce endorsements, faith community endorsements, and local newspaper editorial endorsements that collectively signal community credibility more directly than party affiliation does in a nonpartisan local context. How to Respond to Questions About Party Affiliation

In nonpartisan cities, questions about party affiliation are less common than most candidates expect — because the ballot and the campaign context don't foreground party identity. When voters do ask about party affiliation, the most effective response is direct, honest, and community-focused.

In a nonpartisan city, something like this: "This is a nonpartisan race, which means the ballot doesn't include party labels — and honestly, I think that's right for local government. The question of how to fix our stormwater infrastructure or address housing affordability isn't a Democratic or Republican question. It's a community question, and I've been engaged with this community on those questions for years." This response is honest, explains the nonpartisan structure, and pivots to the substance of the campaign without being evasive.

In a partisan city, where party affiliation is a legitimate voter concern, honesty about your affiliation combined with emphasis on community credentials is the most effective approach. Voters who ask about party affiliation in a local race are usually not asking whether you have the right letter after your name — they are asking whether your values align with theirs on the issues that affect their neighborhood. The candidate who can speak specifically and honestly about those issues, backed by years of civic engagement, is more persuasive on this question than any party credential. Getting people to trust you as a candidate is ultimately more important than party affiliation in the vast majority of local races.

Building Your Own Ground Game Without Party Support

The ground game — the door-knocking, phone-banking, get-out-the-vote operation that party infrastructure typically provides — is the most operationally challenging aspect of running for mayor without a political party. Building it from scratch requires the same foundation as everything else in the 14-year arc: genuine community relationships that produce motivated, personally invested volunteers.

The candidate who has been genuinely engaged in civic life for years arrives at their campaign with a natural volunteer base that a party-backed candidate's institutional operation has to work hard to replicate. The neighborhood association member who has worked alongside the candidate for three years will knock doors with a personal conviction that a recruited party volunteer cannot match. The nonprofit colleague who has served alongside the candidate on a board will make fundraising calls with a credibility that a party phone-banker cannot replicate.

Building a ground game without party support is not a disadvantage when the candidate's civic relationships are genuine and deep. It is simply a different organizational model — one that relies on personal investment rather than institutional obligation, and that therefore produces more motivated and more credible campaign workers.
