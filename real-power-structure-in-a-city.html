The Real Power Structure in a City

Jim BlackburnMarch 20268 min read

The real power structure in a city is almost always different from the official one displayed on org charts and described in city charters — and understanding that difference is one of the most practically important pieces of political intelligence for any future mayor.

Formal authority in city government flows through predictable channels: the city commission has legislative power, the mayor chairs the commission, the city manager administers operations, and department directors manage specific service areas. This formal structure is real and matters. But it describes how power is supposed to work — not how decisions are actually made in practice.

The real power structure in a city includes informal influence networks — the relationships, reputations, and trust patterns that shape decisions before they ever reach a formal vote. Understanding these informal networks is essential preparation for effective governance as well as effective campaigning. According to ICMA research on local governance, the most effective mayors are those who understand and navigate both the formal and informal power structures of their cities — working constructively within them to build the consensus that produces good policy outcomes.

Most candidates who lose to better-prepared opponents lose not because of policy differences or campaign spending gaps — they lose because they did not understand who actually shapes decisions in their city and therefore could not build the relationships that produce winning coalitions. Understanding the real power structure is not optional preparation. It is essential groundwork.

Formal vs. Informal Power — The Critical Distinction

Formal power derives from legal authority — the commission's power to pass ordinances, the mayor's authority to chair meetings and represent the city, the city manager's authority to direct staff. Formal power is constrained by rules, processes, and accountability mechanisms that make it relatively transparent and predictable.

Informal power derives from trust, relationships, and reputation — the ability to shape how people think about an issue before it reaches a formal decision point. The neighborhood association president who mobilizes her 400 members to speak at a commission hearing has informal power that no formal authority confers. The developer who has funded civic organizations and built relationships with commissioners over decades has informal influence that exists entirely outside the formal planning process.

The most practically important implication for a future mayor is that governing effectively requires both formal authority and informal legitimacy. A mayor who wins an election but has not built the informal relationships and trust networks that support their agenda will find that formal authority alone is insufficient to produce results in a council-manager city where every significant decision requires consensus. What a mayor does in a council-manager city is fundamentally about building and maintaining the informal legitimacy that makes formal authority effective.

Who Really Makes Decisions — Elected Officials or Professional Staff

One of the most common misconceptions about local government is that elected officials make all significant decisions. The reality in council-manager cities is more nuanced. Elected officials vote on policy and legislation. Professional staff research, analyze, and recommend — and the recommendations of experienced, trusted professional staff carry enormous weight with commissioners who rely on that expertise to make informed decisions.

A planning director who has worked in the city for fifteen years and built relationships of deep professional trust with the commission is not just implementing decisions — they are substantially shaping what decisions are made through the information and framing they provide. A city manager who prepares the budget recommendation shapes fiscal priorities more concretely than any campaign promise about budget priorities ever will.

This reality has specific implications for future mayors. The candidate who has built genuine professional relationships with key department directors through advisory board service and commission work arrives in office with access to the institutional knowledge that makes effective governance possible from day one. The most important people to know in local politics includes city staff as much as — and sometimes more than — elected officials.

"In a council-manager city, the mayor leads through persuasion and relationship rather than through direct authority. Understanding who needs to be persuaded — and how — requires understanding the real power structure, not just the official one."

The Development Community — The Most Consistent Informal Power

In virtually every growing American city, the development community — real estate developers, construction companies, land use attorneys, and financial institutions that fund development — represents one of the most consistent sources of informal political influence. Development interests require government approvals, and the people who need approvals inevitably build relationships with the people who grant them.

This relationship between development interests and local government is not inherently corrupt — development is necessary for cities to grow, and developers who build housing and infrastructure contribute to the community's material welfare. But the asymmetry of these relationships — where developers have strong financial incentives to invest in political relationships and residents have only civic motivation to counterbalance them — creates systematic pressure toward development-favorable decisions that may not always reflect the broader community interest.

A future mayor who understands this dynamic is neither captured by development interests nor reflexively opposed to them. They engage the development community as one constituency among many, apply consistent standards to development proposals, and maintain relationships with the neighborhood and civic organizations that represent the community interests most directly affected by development decisions. The issues that matter most in mayoral elections — housing, neighborhood character, development pressure — are the policy areas where development community informal influence is most directly felt by residents.

How to Map the Informal Power Structure of Your City

Mapping the informal power structure of your city is an ongoing intelligence-gathering exercise that proceeds through the same channels as all other forms of civic engagement. Attend every city commission meeting and watch who speaks during public comment, who sits in the audience, and who meets with commissioners before and after meetings. The same faces appearing repeatedly around contentious decisions are the informal power network making itself visible.

Build relationships with people who have been active in local civic affairs for many years — former commissioners, longtime nonprofit directors, veteran neighborhood association leaders. These people understand the informal power structure from years of direct observation and participation. Their insight into how decisions actually get made is among the most valuable political education available to a future mayor.

According to the National League of Cities, mayors who understand and navigate the informal power structures of their cities — working constructively with the networks that exist rather than trying to replace them — consistently govern more effectively than those who treat their election as a mandate to restructure the entire civic ecosystem. According to Pew Research Center research on civic networks, the informal influence networks in American cities are most effectively navigated by civic leaders who have built genuine relationships across multiple constituencies. People Also Ask
What is the difference between formal and informal power in city government?
Formal power derives from legal authority — the commission's power to pass ordinances, the mayor's authority to represent the city. Informal power derives from trust, relationships, and reputation — the ability to shape how people think about issues before they reach a formal decision. Both matter. What a mayor does is substantially about managing both types of power simultaneously.
Who really makes decisions in a city — elected officials or professional staff?
Both — in different ways. Elected officials vote on policy. Professional staff research, analyze, and recommend — with enormous practical influence over what decisions are made through the information and framing they provide. The difference between a mayor and city manager explains this dynamic in detail.
How does the development community influence local politics?
Through relationships built around the approval process — developers who need government approvals build relationships with officials and staff who grant them. This creates systematic pressure toward development-favorable decisions. Understanding this dynamic helps future mayors apply consistent, community-centered standards. The issues that matter most in mayoral elections — housing and neighborhood character — are where this influence is most directly felt.
What is a city manager's actual power compared to the mayor's?
In a council-manager city, the city manager has more day-to-day operational authority — supervising all departments, managing thousands of employees, preparing the budget recommendation. The mayor has political authority — chairing the commission, representing the city publicly, setting the legislative agenda. Neither role is more important; both are essential. The difference between a mayor and city manager covers this in full detail.
How do I map the informal power structure of my city?
Attend every commission meeting and observe who appears repeatedly around contentious decisions. Build relationships with people who have been active in civic affairs for many years — their pattern recognition about how decisions get made is invaluable. Notice which organizations generate the most commission responsiveness. The most important people to know in local politics are often the informal power network made visible.
What is a political machine and does my city have one?
A political machine is an organized, durable network that coordinates political activity — endorsements, fundraising, voter mobilization — in exchange for patronage or policy influence. Most mid-size American cities today do not have classic machines, but informal networks of aligned political actors with shared interests exist in most competitive local political environments.
How does money flow through local political relationships?
Through campaign contributions to candidates who share policy preferences. Through donations to civic organizations that build relationships with future elected officials. Through the economic activity that employment, contracts, and development approvals create for politically connected businesses. How to fundraise covers building a broad donor base that reduces financial dependence on any single constituency.
How do I navigate the real power structure without being captured by it?
By building relationships across all constituencies rather than becoming dependent on any single one. By maintaining consistency between what you say to different groups. By prioritizing community interest over donor interest when they conflict. Building a political coalition across diverse constituencies is the structural protection against capture by any single power network.
