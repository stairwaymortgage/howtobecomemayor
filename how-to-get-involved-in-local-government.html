How to Get Involved in Local Government

Jim BlackburnMarch 20268 min read

Getting involved in local government is one of the most accessible civic acts available to any American resident — and for the future mayor, it is the foundational strategy of the entire path to elected leadership.

Getting involved in local government is one of the most accessible and most overlooked civic acts available to any American resident. You do not need an invitation, a political connection, or a title. Every city commission meeting is open to the public. Every city advisory board accepts applications from residents. Every neighborhood association welcomes new members. The doors to local government are open — most people simply never walk through them.

For the future mayor, getting involved in local government is not just a civic obligation. It is the foundational strategy of the entire path to elected leadership. The person who has been genuinely engaged in their city's governance for eight years before running for office arrives at their campaign with something no amount of money can manufacture — a community that already knows them, trusts them, and has watched them serve.

According to the National League of Cities, most Americans interact with local government only when something goes wrong — when a road floods, when a permit gets denied, when a development project appears next door. The future civic leader who engages before anything goes wrong builds a different and more powerful relationship with their city than the reactive resident who shows up only in crisis.

Start With the City Commission Meeting

The most important first step in getting involved in local government is attending your city commission meeting. This is the regular public gathering — typically held two to four times per month — where elected commissioners vote on city legislation, budgets, zoning decisions, and policy matters that affect every resident.

Commission meetings are free and open to the public. Most cities broadcast them online. Most allow residents to sign up for public comment and speak for two to three minutes on any agenda item or on general city topics. That public comment opportunity is more powerful than most residents realize — it puts your name on the official record of city proceedings, makes you visible to commissioners and city staff, and positions you as a resident who engages rather than one who only watches.

Getting involved in local government at the commission meeting level is the most direct education available in how your city actually makes decisions — who has influence, which issues generate the most controversy, which commissioners are most engaged, and where the gaps in civic leadership are. The first step to becoming mayor begins precisely here.

City Advisory Boards — Your First Official Role

Every city appoints residents to serve on advisory boards and committees that advise the commission on specific areas of city policy. Common advisory boards include planning and zoning, parks and recreation, sustainability and environmental quality, budget and finance, historic preservation, marine and waterways, and civil service. These are unpaid positions that typically require an application, occasional interviews, and a commitment to attend regular meetings.

Serving on a city advisory board is one of the most valuable things a future mayor can do in the early years of their civic journey. It accomplishes several things simultaneously. You learn how city policy is actually developed — from staff research and analysis through committee review to commission vote. You build relationships with city staff who will remember your name and your contributions for years. Commissioners begin to associate your name with engaged, informed civic participation. And you develop subject-matter expertise in the specific policy area your board covers.

According to the International City/County Management Association, advisory boards serve as the primary formal channel through which resident expertise informs professional city staff recommendations. That makes board service not just a civic credential but a genuine contribution to better city governance. Here is exactly how to apply for and join a city advisory board in your city.

"The future mayor who has served on three city advisory boards over six years arrives at their campaign with something no political consultant can create — institutional credibility earned through genuine service."

Neighborhood Associations — The Most Underrated Power in Local Politics

In cities across America, neighborhood associations are among the most influential organizations in local civic life. They host candidate forums that shape voter perceptions. They issue endorsements that mobilize hundreds of engaged voters. Their leaders know each other, communicate regularly, and can activate their networks on short notice for or against a development proposal, a policy change, or a political candidate.

Getting involved in local government through neighborhood associations is different from advisory board service in one important way — neighborhood associations are community organizations, not government bodies. They operate independently of city hall, which means the relationships you build through them are community relationships rather than institutional ones. Those community relationships are the foundation of the coalition that eventually elects a mayor.

Find your neighborhood association through your city's website — most cities maintain a directory of recognized neighborhood organizations. Attend their meetings regularly before you have any agenda. Contribute ideas. Volunteer for initiatives. Build relationships with leaders who have deep roots in the community. A single neighborhood association president who genuinely believes in you is worth more to a mayoral campaign than ten thousand social media followers. How neighborhood associations influence elections is one of the most important things a future mayor can understand.

Volunteering for Local Causes — Building Reputation Through Service

Beyond formal government structures, getting involved in local government means getting involved in the civic ecosystem that surrounds it — the nonprofits, community initiatives, local advocacy organizations, and grassroots movements that do much of the actual work of making cities better.

Volunteering for a local cause related to your signature issue — flood mitigation, housing advocacy, neighborhood preservation, environmental resilience — does three things for a future mayor. It builds relationships with the organized groups most engaged on that issue. It develops genuine expertise and empathy that no amount of reading can replicate. And it creates a public record of contribution that long predates your campaign announcement.

Research from the Pew Research Center shows that civic volunteers are significantly more likely to vote in local elections and significantly more likely to trust and endorse candidates they have worked alongside than candidates they have only seen on mailers. Getting involved in local government through volunteer work is not just good for your community — it is strategically essential for the future mayor building the coalition that wins. People Also Ask
What local government volunteer opportunities exist for regular citizens?
City advisory boards, neighborhood association leadership, nonprofit boards, community planning initiatives, and public comment participation at commission meetings are all available to any resident. Joining a city advisory board is the most direct formal role available to someone with no prior civic engagement.
How do I find out what advisory boards my city has?
Check your city's official website under City Clerk, City Manager, or a dedicated Boards and Commissions page. Most cities publish a complete list of advisory boards along with current vacancies, application processes, and meeting schedules. Call the city clerk's office directly if you cannot find the information online.
What is the difference between a city advisory board and a city commission?
A city commission is an elected body with legal authority to pass ordinances, approve budgets, and make binding policy decisions. A city advisory board is an appointed body of residents that advises the commission and city staff but has no binding authority. Advisory boards feed recommendations upward. Commissions make the official decisions. Understanding what a mayor does includes understanding both layers.
What should I say when I speak at a public city commission meeting?
Be brief, specific, and constructive. State your name and your neighborhood. Reference the specific agenda item or issue you are addressing. Make one clear point. Offer a concrete suggestion if you have one. Thank the commissioners for their time. You have two to three minutes — use them deliberately. Your goal is to be remembered as informed and respectful, not as the person who lectures.
How do neighborhood associations connect to city government?
Neighborhood associations serve as organized community voices in city planning, zoning, and development processes. They participate in public hearings, submit formal comments on proposed projects, and maintain relationships with commissioners and city staff that give organized neighborhood concerns significant weight in city decisions. Their influence on elections is equally significant.
What is a planning and zoning board and should I join one?
A planning and zoning board advises the city commission on land use decisions — development approvals, zoning changes, comprehensive plan amendments, and related matters. It is one of the most consequential advisory boards in any city because land use decisions touch property values, neighborhood character, housing supply, and environmental quality. For a future mayor interested in housing, development, or neighborhood preservation, it is an excellent first advisory board. The issues that matter most in mayoral elections often flow directly from planning and zoning decisions.
How do I build relationships with city officials before running for office?
Show up consistently at public meetings over a period of years. Ask thoughtful questions. Serve on advisory boards. Contribute genuine expertise on issues you have studied. Treat city staff with respect — they have long memories and significant informal influence. The future mayor who has worked constructively alongside city officials for years arrives at their campaign with a network that cannot be built in a campaign season. The most important people to know in local politics includes city staff as much as elected officials.
What is the most important thing a future mayor can do in their first year of civic engagement?
Show up consistently without asking for anything. Attend meetings. Join your neighborhood association. Apply for an advisory board. Begin publishing what you are learning publicly every week. Do all of this before you have any official title or any campaign to promote. The first year of civic engagement is about becoming a familiar, trusted presence in your city's civic community. The first step to becoming mayor is the same as the first step to genuine community leadership.
