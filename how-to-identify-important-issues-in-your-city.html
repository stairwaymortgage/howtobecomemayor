How to Identify Important Issues in Your City

Jim BlackburnMarch 20268 min read

Identifying the most important issues in your city is one of the most foundational research tasks that future mayors undertake — and the candidates who win are consistently those who have done this research systematically for years before the campaign begins.

Identifying the most important issues in your city is one of the most foundational — and most underperformed — research tasks that future mayors undertake. Most candidates arrive at their issue positions through a combination of personal intuition, informal conversation, and campaign-season polling. The candidates who win consistently are those who have done the systematic, sustained research that produces genuine expertise on their city’s most pressing challenges — not just awareness, but deep understanding of the causes, costs, and realistic solutions to the specific problems their community faces.

The good news is that virtually all of the information you need to identify your city’s most important issues is publicly available, free, and accessible to any resident who is willing to look for it. Your city produces hundreds of documents every year that, read carefully and systematically, paint a detailed picture of what it is actually struggling with — as distinct from what it says its priorities are in mission statements and strategic plans.

According to the National League of Cities State of the Cities report, the gap between what mayors say their priorities are and what residents identify as their most pressing concerns is one of the most consistent findings in urban governance research. Identifying the issues that matter most in your city requires going beyond official statements to the actual experience of residents in different neighborhoods and demographics.

Reading the Budget — Where the Real Priorities Live

Your city’s annual budget is the single most honest document your city government produces. The budget does not say what the city wants to prioritize — it shows what it actually prioritizes, in the form of specific dollar allocations to specific departments, programs, and infrastructure investments.

Reading the budget for the first time is disorienting — it is a complex document that requires familiarity with government accounting formats to interpret correctly. But the effort is well worth it. The budget reveals things that no other document will: which departments are chronically underfunded relative to their responsibilities. Which capital projects have been deferred year after year for lack of funding. Which programs are growing and which are shrinking. Where the city is choosing to spend money when choices must be made.

Comparing the budget to the strategic plan is particularly revealing. When the strategic plan identifies five priority areas but the budget increases funding in only two of them, the three underfunded priorities are where community frustration is most likely to exist — and where a well-prepared candidate with genuine solutions has the most political opportunity. The issues that matter most in mayoral elections are often the ones that consistently appear in the budget as underfunded relative to their stated importance.

Commission Meeting Agendas — The Real-Time Issue Map

Your city commission meeting agendas and minutes are a real-time map of what your city is actually wrestling with. Every item that appears on a commission agenda represents an issue that has risen to the level of requiring elected official attention — either because it has reached a decision point or because community pressure has demanded it.

Reading two years of commission meeting agendas reveals patterns that are invisible in any single meeting. Which issues come up repeatedly? Which items generate the most public comment? Which staff reports are brought back multiple times because the commission cannot reach a decision? Which departments appear most frequently with requests for additional resources or policy direction? These patterns are the signature of the issues your city is genuinely struggling with — as distinct from the issues it is managing successfully.

The public comment record is particularly valuable. The residents who show up at commission meetings to speak during public comment are the most civically engaged segment of your future voter base. What are they angry about? What are they afraid of? What have they been asking for that they have not received? Understanding this record — which is publicly available in meeting minutes on your city’s website — is a direct window into the concerns of your most politically active constituency.

"The city’s budget tells you what it is actually spending money on. The commission meeting minutes tell you what residents are actually angry about. The gap between these two documents is your political opportunity map."

Neighborhood Research — Understanding How Issues Vary by Geography

One of the most common mistakes in mayoral issue research is treating the city as a uniform entity. Cities are not uniform — they are collections of distinct neighborhoods with different demographics, different histories, different vulnerabilities, and different community priorities. The flooding that is the dominant issue in one neighborhood may be completely irrelevant in another. The development pressure transforming one district may be invisible in a different part of the city.

Understanding how issues vary by neighborhood requires attending neighborhood association meetings across different parts of the city — not just your home neighborhood. Spending one evening per month at a different neighborhood’s association meeting over the course of a year gives you a comprehensive, community-sourced map of the issues that different constituencies care most about. This map is more actionable than any polling data and more credible than any consultant’s issue analysis because it comes directly from the residents who live with these issues every day.

According to ICMA research on community engagement, mayoral candidates who demonstrate specific knowledge of issues affecting different neighborhoods — not just citywide patterns — consistently outperform candidates who offer only city-wide generalizations. The voter in a flood-prone neighborhood who hears a candidate speak specifically about the stormwater capacity issues in their specific area is responding to something genuinely different from the candidate who talks about infrastructure in the abstract. Getting involved in local government across different neighborhoods over years is how this specific, granular knowledge is built.

Turning Local Problems Into a Political Platform

The transition from identifying important issues to building a political platform around them requires a specific kind of thinking that goes beyond diagnosis to prescription. Identifying that housing costs are unaffordable is not a platform. Understanding specifically how restrictive zoning, inadequate infrastructure investment, and limited affordable housing funding interact to produce the affordability crisis in your city — and having a specific set of policy proposals that address each contributing factor — is a platform.

Building a substantive policy platform takes years of engaged research and civic participation. The future mayor who has been attending planning and zoning board meetings for four years, reading every housing study the city has commissioned, and engaging with affordable housing advocates and neighborhood association leaders has the raw material for a genuinely substantive housing platform. The candidate who develops their housing positions in a campaign planning meeting does not.

The practical implication is that platform development should begin in the first year of civic engagement — not the first month of campaign planning. Every year of systematic issue research adds depth to the platform that will eventually define your candidacy. Building credibility to run for office through issue expertise is the same process as building the platform you will run on. How To Become Mayor structures the issue research process as a deliberate part of the 14-year arc — and the community behind it is joined by application.

According to Pew Research Center data on civic engagement, residents who participate actively in local civic life — attending meetings, serving on boards, contributing to community organizations — have significantly more detailed and accurate knowledge of local policy challenges than residents who engage primarily through news media or social media. Active civic participation is the research method that produces the best issue knowledge for a future mayor.

People Also Ask
How do I research what my city’s biggest problems are?
Read your city’s annual budget, strategic plan, and two years of commission meeting agendas and minutes. Attend neighborhood association meetings across different parts of the city. Talk directly with residents, business owners, and civic leaders. These primary sources are more reliable than polling and more actionable than consultant analysis. What issues matter most in a mayoral election covers the five most common categories.
What does a city budget tell me about local priorities?
Everything that matters. The budget shows what the city is actually spending money on — as distinct from what it says it is prioritizing. Comparing the budget to the strategic plan reveals where stated priorities are underfunded, which is often where community frustration is most acute and political opportunity is greatest. Reading the budget is one of the most valuable first-year civic engagement activities for any future mayor. The first step to becoming mayor includes reading the city budget.
How do I read a city strategic plan?
A city strategic plan is a multi-year document that identifies the city’s stated priorities and goals. Read it as a statement of intention rather than a record of achievement. Then compare it to the budget to see which stated priorities have received funding and which have not. The gap between intention and funding is where real civic problems often live — and where genuine policy solutions have the most impact.
What data sources exist for understanding my city’s civic challenges?
City budget and financial reports. Commission meeting agendas and minutes. City comprehensive plan and strategic plan. State and federal infrastructure condition reports. U.S. Census Bureau data on population, housing, and economic trends. Local newspaper archives. Neighborhood association meeting minutes. U.S. Census Bureau data is particularly valuable for understanding demographic and housing trends.
How do neighborhood associations identify the issues that matter most?
Through regular meetings where residents discuss their experiences and concerns, through engagement with city planning processes that affect their neighborhood, and through direct interaction with city staff and elected officials at community events and hearings. Attending neighborhood association meetings across different parts of the city provides a ground-level view of how issues vary by geography that no citywide data source can replicate. How neighborhood associations influence elections explains their role in translating community concerns into electoral outcomes.
What is the best way to conduct informal community research?
Show up at the places where your community already gathers — neighborhood association meetings, community festivals, faith organization events, local business gatherings — and listen more than you talk. Ask open-ended questions: what do you wish was different about living here? What are you most worried about? What has been frustrating you about the city? These conversations produce insights that formal research methods often miss. Becoming known in your community through years of genuine engagement is the ongoing version of this research.
How do I turn a local problem into a political platform?
By moving from diagnosis to specific prescription. Identifying a problem is not a platform. Understanding the specific causes, costs, and realistic policy solutions to that problem — and being able to articulate them clearly — is a platform. This depth comes from years of systematic research, not campaign planning sessions. The issues that matter most in mayoral elections and this post together form the research and platform development framework.
What makes a local issue worth building a campaign around?
Three things: it affects a large number of engaged voters personally and directly, there are genuine policy solutions that a mayor can actually implement, and you have developed enough genuine expertise on it to speak credibly and specifically. An issue that affects many people but has no realistic mayoral solution is a complaint, not a platform. An issue you understand only superficially is a vulnerability. Building credibility to run for office through deep issue expertise is what transforms a concern into a credible campaign platform.
