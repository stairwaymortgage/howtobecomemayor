Difference Between Mayor and City Manager

Jim BlackburnMarch 20268 min read

The difference between a mayor and a city manager is one of the most practically important things a future mayor can understand — and one of the least-taught distinctions in civic education. In the majority of American cities, these two roles are completely different and must work in genuine partnership for city government to be effective.

The difference between a mayor and a city manager is one of the most practically important things a future mayor can understand — and one of the least-taught distinctions in civic education. In cities that use the council-manager form of government, which accounts for the majority of American municipalities, the mayor and the city manager play fundamentally different roles that require completely different skills and operate under completely different accountabilities.

Understanding this difference is not just academic. It shapes how you campaign for mayor, what you promise voters, how you govern once elected, and what success looks like in the job. The future mayor who misunderstands this relationship often promises things they cannot deliver and fails to leverage the partnership that makes council-manager government work at its best.

According to the International City/County Management Association, approximately 4,000 American cities — including the majority of cities between 25,000 and 500,000 residents — use the council-manager form of government. In these cities, the distinction between the mayor and the city manager is the central structural feature of how city hall works.

The Mayor — Elected, Political, and Legislative

U.S. Census Bureau data shows there are 19,495 incorporated municipalities in America — the vast majority of mid-size cities among them use the council-manager model, making this distinction essential knowledge for any future mayor. The mayor is an elected official. They are chosen by voters, accountable to voters, and serve a fixed term defined by the city charter. In a council-manager city, the mayor is one vote among five, six, or seven on the city commission. They chair commission meetings, represent the city at public events and in regional relationships, set the legislative agenda in partnership with fellow commissioners, and serve as the public face of city government.

What the mayor in a council-manager city does not do is manage city operations. They do not supervise department heads. They do not direct city staff to take specific actions. They do not have unilateral authority to spend city money or make binding commitments on behalf of the city. All of those functions belong to the city manager — a professional administrator hired by the commission, not elected by voters.

The mayor's power in a council-manager system is primarily the power of persuasion — the ability to build consensus among commissioners, communicate a compelling vision to the public, and maintain the kind of community trust that gives elected leadership moral authority even when formal authority is limited. This is why building credibility to run for office matters so much. The credibility that wins the election is the same credibility that makes the governance effective.

The City Manager — Appointed, Professional, and Administrative

The city manager is a professional administrator appointed by and accountable to the city commission. They hold a graduate degree in public administration or a related field, typically have ten to twenty years of progressively responsible experience in local government management, and are hired through a competitive national search process.

The city manager's responsibilities are operational and administrative. They supervise all city department heads — the police chief, fire chief, planning director, public works director, parks director, and others. They prepare the city's budget for commission approval. They implement the policies adopted by the commission. They manage thousands of city employees and oversee the delivery of every service the city provides.

The city manager serves at the pleasure of the commission — meaning a majority of the commission can terminate the manager's employment at any time, for any reason. In practice, city managers in stable, well-governed cities often serve for five to fifteen years or more, providing continuity of professional management across multiple elected commission compositions. The difference between a mayor and a city manager includes this continuity — the manager provides institutional memory and operational stability that elected officials, who come and go on election cycles, cannot.

"The best outcomes in council-manager cities happen when the mayor provides visionary political leadership and the city manager provides expert professional execution — neither trying to do the other's job."

How the Partnership Works — and When It Fails

The mayor-manager relationship works best when both parties understand and respect each other's distinct roles. The mayor sets policy direction and community priorities. The manager determines the most effective way to implement that direction through professional staff. The mayor communicates with the public about the city's vision and values. The manager communicates with the commission about operational realities and resource constraints.

The partnership fails in predictable ways. A mayor who tries to micromanage department heads — bypassing the manager and directing staff to take specific operational actions — undermines the manager's authority and creates organizational confusion that reduces government effectiveness. A city manager who tries to set policy direction rather than implement it — making political decisions that properly belong to elected officials — oversteps the appropriate professional role and damages the democratic accountability that elected government is supposed to provide.

The most successful mayors in council-manager cities are those who come into office with a clear policy vision, strong community relationships, and a genuine understanding of and respect for professional city management. They work with the manager as a partner, not a subordinate. They leverage the manager's expertise to implement their policy priorities more effectively than they could alone. And they hold the manager accountable for results without trying to dictate methods. Understanding the real power structure in a city includes this partnership at its center.

Why This Matters for Future Mayors

The difference between a mayor and a city manager matters for future mayors in three specific practical ways.

First, it shapes what you can honestly promise voters. A mayor in a council-manager city cannot promise to fire the police chief, reorganize a city department, or redirect departmental spending without commission approval. Candidates who make operational promises they lack the authority to keep damage their credibility when they cannot deliver.

Second, it shapes how you campaign. A future mayor in a council-manager city should campaign on policy vision, community values, and coalition-building capacity — not on managerial efficiency or operational expertise. The operational expertise belongs to the manager. What voters are actually choosing is political leadership, community representation, and the judgment to hire and hold accountable a professional city manager.

Third, it shapes the skills you need to develop in the years before you run. The skills of an effective council-manager mayor — persuasion, coalition-building, community trust, clear public communication — are exactly the skills that building a political coalition over years of civic engagement develops. How To Become Mayor is built around this reality — preparing future mayors for the job as it actually exists, not as it is misunderstood.

According to the National League of Cities, cities with stable, productive mayor-manager relationships consistently outperform cities where that relationship is contentious or confused on virtually every measure of municipal effectiveness. Understanding the difference between a mayor and a city manager is not just organizational knowledge. It is the foundation of effective governance.

People Also Ask
Which cities have a city manager instead of a strong mayor?
The majority of American cities between 25,000 and 500,000 residents use the council-manager form of government with a professional city manager. Fort Lauderdale, San Jose, Phoenix, Dallas, and hundreds of other mid-size and large cities use this model. How local elections work differs significantly between strong-mayor and council-manager cities.
Who has more power — the mayor or the city manager?
It depends on the form of government. In a strong-mayor city, the mayor has more power — direct executive authority over city operations. In a council-manager city, the city manager often has more day-to-day operational authority — supervising all departments and managing thousands of employees — while the mayor has political and legislative authority that the manager lacks entirely.
Can a mayor fire a city manager?
Not alone — in a council-manager city, the city manager serves at the pleasure of the commission as a whole. A majority vote of the commission is required to terminate or hire the city manager. A single commissioner including the mayor cannot unilaterally remove the manager. This is an important constraint that future mayors should understand before they make promises about personnel changes.
What does a city manager do on a daily basis?
A city manager supervises all department heads, manages the day-to-day operations of city government, prepares budget proposals for commission approval, implements policies adopted by the commission, handles intergovernmental relationships, and responds to operational issues and crises as they arise. What a mayor does in contrast is primarily political and legislative rather than operational.
Why do some cities have city managers instead of strong mayors?
The council-manager form of government was developed in the early 20th century as a reform model to professionalize city administration and reduce political patronage. The idea is that routine city operations should be managed by trained professionals accountable to performance standards rather than by elected officials accountable to political constituencies. Most mid-size cities adopted this model during the municipal reform era of the 1920s through 1950s.
How does the council-manager system affect how a mayor campaigns?
A mayor running in a council-manager city should campaign on policy vision, community values, and coalition-building capacity — not on operational efficiency or management expertise. What voters are choosing is political and community leadership, not a city administrator. The issues that matter most in mayoral elections in council-manager cities tend to be policy and values questions rather than administrative ones.
What percentage of American cities use the council-manager system?
Approximately 60 to 65 percent of American cities use the council-manager or related forms of professional management government, according to ICMA research. This percentage is higher among mid-size cities — the 25,000 to 500,000 population range — where professional management was most aggressively adopted during the municipal reform era.
How does a mayor build influence in a council-manager city?
Through the same mechanisms that build influence in any collaborative organization — genuine relationships with fellow commissioners, public trust earned through years of visible community engagement, clear and compelling communication of a policy vision, and the credibility that comes from doing what you say you will do consistently over time. Building credibility to run for office also builds the influence needed to govern effectively.
